# HF-02 — Reading a Creatine Listing After 40: Ingredient, Serving, and Unknowns

## Hook

A creatine label can look precise because it names a serving and a powder. Precision on the package is not the same as a personal conclusion. Read the ingredient question, the listing facts, and the unknowns as three separate layers

## Context

This article uses THORNE Creatine, micronized creatine monohydrate powder, unflavored, 90 servings, Pack of 1, ASIN B07978VPPH, as an educational listing example. Rick’s readback recorded the title’s 5 g per serving and 90-serving language, the powder format, and the manufacturer field on 2026-09-12 [S1]. These are E2 observations from the listing snapshot.

The NIH Office of Dietary Supplements explains that exercise and performance supplements need to be considered by ingredient, evidence, context, and possible concerns rather than by marketing language alone [S2]. A review of creatine and aging-related questions can provide general context, but research on an ingredient or protocol does not validate a particular SKU [S3]. This draft makes that separation explicit.

## Layer one: what the listing identifies

Record the exact product name, ASIN, flavor, package, and serving language. For the THORNE example, the readback records micronized creatine monohydrate, unflavored powder, 5 g per serving, and 90 servings [S1]. The full ingredient panel was not fully transcribed, and the physical label, lot, seal, and an independent certificate record were not verified.

A listing can therefore support a careful sentence such as: the product page displayed a 5 g serving and 90 servings in the recorded variant on 2026-09-12. It cannot support a broader statement about what every package contains today. Formula, seller, and page data can change, so the date stays attached to the observation.

## Layer two: what evidence can say generally

An independent source can discuss creatine research, study populations, protocols, and limits. The ODS fact sheet is useful for the general supplement-evidence frame [S2]. The review in the project corpus discusses creatine and cognition-related questions in older adults, but that context must remain at the level of the studied ingredient or protocol [S3]. It is not proof for this product and is not a forecast for a person over 40.

Keep the population, dose, duration, comparison, and measured outcome beside any general statement. Do not turn “the literature examined” into “this listing produces.” Do not infer a universal serving from a label. Individual context, medicines, health history, and professional advice can change the question.

## Layer three: claims from the seller

Rick’s readback reports that the listing uses language about lean muscle mass, endurance, cognitive function, cellular energy, and brain function [S1]. If these statements are mentioned, identify them as claims from the seller or product listing, class E2. They are not independent clinical proof.

As of 2026-09-12, no completed standardized independent study confirming those claims for this product was located or verified within this review scope. That limitation is specific to the SKU and search scope. It does not declare that no research exists anywhere. The responsible editorial move is to keep the claim attributed, avoid turning it into a personal result, and state the uncertainty near the claim.

The listing also displays a certification-related statement about NSF Certified for Sport and testing for label claims and banned substances [S1]. This is a listing declaration in the readback, not a clinical approval, universal safety conclusion, or proof of performance. A certificate record and current package would require separate verification.

## A practical label checklist

Write down the form, flavor, serving statement, number of servings, complete ingredient panel, directions, warnings, seller, and date. Mark every field that was not checked as not verified. Ask whether the purpose is convenience, label literacy, or a question for a clinician or dietitian.

Do not use this draft to decide whether a supplement is appropriate for a diagnosed condition, medication pattern, or individual goal. If a professional conversation is needed, bring the exact label and the question. ODS provides general information, not a personal decision [S2].

## A note on precision

Numbers on a label can create a false sense that the decision is complete. A serving number answers only what the page reports. It does not answer whether the panel is current, whether the package is authentic, whether the research protocol matches the listing, or whether the ingredient fits a person’s circumstances. Keep those questions visible instead of using precision as a shortcut.

## Check the question before the number

A serving field can be useful for identifying a package, but it cannot answer whether supplementation belongs in a person’s plan. Ask what decision the number is meant to inform and what information is still missing. Keep the current label, the reason for considering the ingredient, and any professional question together. That record is more responsible than treating one precise field as a complete answer.

## Closing

Creatine label literacy means keeping the SKU, the ingredient research, and the seller’s language in separate boxes. THORNE is an E2 example for reading a dated listing, not proof of a result. The useful conclusion is a better question and a visible unknown, not a promise
